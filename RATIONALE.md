# Rationale

The exercise: read a made-up product scenario, then commit to two questions you'd ask and two actions you'd take. Two minutes. No Figma, no deliverable. A board comes back with the strongest questions and actions, and any of yours that hit are marked.

Everything below is a decision made deliberately, usually after a version that didn't work.

---

## It has to feel like an activity, not a lesson

This is the constraint everything else bends around.

The first version ended with a critique. I'd answer, then get told what I'd overlooked. Accurate, useful, and unpleasant enough that I wouldn't have done it twice.

So the board compares, it doesn't score. Hitting any entry is a hit. Hitting none isn't a failure, and nothing gets marked wrong.

---

## Two questions, two actions

The obvious version asks for every question you'd want to ask. It's worse. Listing is easy — you write eight, none of them cost anything, and you never have to decide.

The other extreme, exactly one question, was too tight. It made the whole thing feel like a single shot you could waste.

Two and two holds. Enough room that you're not agonizing, tight enough that you're choosing. And it's symmetrical, which matters more than it should — it's the version people repeat without looking it up.

The actions are there for a specific reason. Constraints don't change what you'd want to *know*; they change what you can actually do. Drop the actions and the constraint becomes decoration.

An action is not a solution. "Interview the people who left" isn't a design. It's the next move, and picking a good one under a real constraint is the senior skill.

---

## Never state the problem

Nobody hands a designer a framed problem. They hand you numbers and somebody's opinion about what the numbers mean. Figuring out what's actually wrong is the job.

So the scenario reproduces that: a seat at the table and a colleague with a position. Never a problem statement.

I got this wrong twice before it worked.

- "The team's OKR is to reduce time-to-exam" tells you what to optimize. Now everyone answers the same question.
- "This landed on your desk Monday" has nobody attached to it. It reads as a slide, not a job.
- "Your PM sends you these numbers. She thinks the picker is working and the drop-off is a marketing problem. You're not sure." — this one works.

A named person with a stated position gives you something to disagree with. It also keeps the best first move available: *is this even the problem?*

---

## Three scenario modes

The flaw I ran into after four rounds: every board was telling me to go do research. Not because research is wrong, but because I'd built four discovery scenarios in a row without noticing. If the evidence is missing along with the diagnosis, "go find out" is the only legal move.

So there are three modes, and they rotate.

**Discovery.** Unexplained numbers, no research done. Actions are about what to learn and from whom. The default, and the one to stop over-serving.

**Interpretation.** The research is already in and you get handed it — exit interviews, site visit findings, a survey. Two readings fit the same evidence and imply opposite products. Questions become about interpretation; actions become real moves. The constraint has to *close the research door* here, not narrow it: you present Friday, the vendor contract is closed, the panel won't run again this quarter.

**Commitment.** The decision is made and announced. You shape how it lands, not whether it happens. This kills research as an escape entirely and tests whether you can do good work inside someone else's call.

---

## The board has two halves

Six questions, four actions.

Four actions rather than six because a working constraint leaves genuinely fewer legitimate moves. That asymmetry doubles as a build-time check: **if you can't fill four distinct actions, the scenario is underspecified.** The user doesn't have enough to move on, and every action will collapse into another study.

Second check, learned the hard way: if every action on the board is a study, you've built a discovery scenario whether you meant to or not.

---

## What the board actually is

Not crowd data, and not the six best questions a designer could ask. **They're the questions the scenario was built around.**

Constructing a scenario means planting the evidence that reveals the problem — the branch where people fall out, the metric measured on the wrong population, the threshold sitting in plain sight. Each planted thing is a board slot. The board is the list of what got buried and where.

Saying that plainly is more useful than implying a popularity that doesn't exist. It tells you what a miss means: not a failure to think well, just a plant you didn't spot.

The cost is circularity — a board can only contain what its author thought of, so a genuinely novel angle would read as a miss. That's why "if it's good, it goes on the board" isn't a footnote. It's the release valve, and it's what keeps the whole construction honest.

---

## The dud

One entry is a question that sounds smart and isn't — usually one that jumps to a fix, or one the constraint already ruled out. Often it's the one a stakeholder in the scenario has already endorsed, which is what makes it seductive.

It's there because that failure has nothing to do with knowledge. Six good questions don't make the pattern visible. Labeling one does.

I'm not certain about it. It works alone, where nobody has to own the dud but you. It would not work on a public board built from other people's answers — marking a real person's contribution as the bad one is exactly the feeling this thing exists to avoid. Solo only. If it doesn't earn its place, cut it.

---

## End on a pattern, not a correction

Every round closes with one named pattern and how to spot it next time. Two lines.

> **Two populations, one number.** When a metric compares people who did X against people who didn't, check whether X selected them before you credit X. Spot it by asking who *couldn't* have been in the winning group.

The name is the point. Scenario facts expire the moment you close the app; a named pattern travels into a real meeting. Anything you'd have pulled that the user didn't goes right before this, in two lines — long paragraphs of what-you-missed turn the ending into the critique the whole thing is built to avoid, no matter how kindly they're worded.

---

## Some things are left undefined on purpose

Most scenarios have a gap planted in them. A metric that's named but never defined — "dwell time hasn't moved in eighteen months," started when, ended when, nobody says. A threshold with no stated basis. A step whose logic doesn't quite follow.

Noticing that a term was never defined is one of the most valuable questions anyone asks in a real meeting, and it only gets practiced if something is actually missing.

The line I hold: **undefined, not incorrect.** A wrong fact makes you distrust every other fact, and then you stop reasoning and start proofreading. A gap produces the same useful head-scratch without poisoning the rest.

## Three facts, not five

Early scenarios carried five or six data points. This is usually read on a phone with nothing to write on, and a scenario you can't hold in your head becomes an exercise in scrolling.

Three is enough to create tension. The fourth is usually me showing my work.

---

## What I cut

**Scale.** Early versions specified users and volume — 9,000 stores, four million transactions a week. It changed nothing about how anyone reasons. Assume the product has users.

**Your role and title.** Cut, with a correction. Seniority doesn't change the exercise. *Permission* does — whether you can walk into the founder's office or have to build a case over six weeks completely changes what a good action is. So the field went away and permission moved into the constraint, where it belonged.

**The second design principle.** Scenarios used to state two. Two lets you skip both. One principle, chosen because it's the one the scenario quietly violates, makes ignoring it a visible choice.

---

## The numbers cut both ways

If everything is going badly, the answer is obvious. Every scenario carries a real win alongside the real cost — throughput up twelve percent *and* complaints up; customer repeat rate at an all-time high *and* the best contractors quitting. Without that tension you get a bug report, not a judgment call.

---

## The constraint is structural, not a difficult person

"The stakeholder is being unreasonable" is a mood. A frozen travel budget, a six-week legal review, contractor classification rules that forbid setting anyone's hours, a rollout already under contract — those are constraints. They block the obvious fix and force the interesting move sideways.

It's also where the pressure comes from. It's what makes a scenario feel like a Tuesday instead of a case study.

---

## The flow diagram earns its place

Not decoration. A flow you can see is a flow you can poke holes in, and it gives you something to point at.

**Show the branch where people fall out.** The drop-off path is usually where the real question lives.

**Put thresholds in the flow, visible.** "Estimate under $2,500?" with both paths drawn. A threshold is a design decision sitting in plain sight, and about half the time it *is* the problem.

**Stack the branches, never place them side by side.** This gets read on a phone. Side-by-side halves the width per box and the text shrinks until you have to zoom — and a flow you have to zoom into isn't doing its job. Vertical space is free. Branches that rejoin become full-width rows off a left rail; branches that genuinely diverge each get a labeled lane with their own steps beneath.

Stacking has a second benefit I didn't anticipate. Lanes are visually comparable, so asymmetry jumps out — if one answer gets four steps and another gets one, you can see that the product invested in one path and abandoned another. That's often the finding.

Which makes the construction of the flow a legitimate answer in itself. "Why is this a branch instead of a merge?" and "could this step happen after payment instead of before?" are often better than anything about the metrics.

---

## Answers are allowed to be inconclusive

If you keep going past the board, you can ask your questions and get answers — from a data scientist, an ops lead, a researcher, in their own voice.

Not every answer advances the plot. Real ones don't. Sometimes the number you asked for is only measured on the population that didn't have the problem, and nobody can tell you about the rest. If every question moves things forward, it stops being a scenario and becomes a puzzle — and puzzles have a right answer you can miss.

That's also why it isn't called a puzzle.

---

## Rotate the shape, not the industry

The thing I got most wrong.

I ran a pharmacy queue, a vet clinic check-in, and an insurance claims flow. Three industries, three scenarios that looked completely different, and **all three were the same problem underneath**: a system took over a judgment a human used to make, the target metric improved, and the cost landed somewhere the metric can't see.

Variety of setting hides sameness of problem. You'd get very good at one thing while feeling like you were practicing everything.

Settings are unlimited. Problem shapes are maybe thirty or forty, and the shape is what you're actually training. The skill keeps a list and rotates deliberately — two users who want opposite things from one interface, the workaround that's better than the product, the bottleneck that just moved downstream, the thing that only works at the good sites and nobody's asking why.

---

## How it was built

The scenarios are generated with Claude from the template in `SKILL.md`. That's the point of publishing it as a skill rather than a blog post — you get the generator, not my examples.

They're invented. Fictional companies, fictional numbers. Partly so nobody's real product gets discussed with made-up metrics attached, and partly because a scenario tuned to teach one thing beats a real one that's messy in less useful ways.

And they're hypothetical, which matters if you're interviewing. A sharp answer here is a point of view. It isn't a story about something you did, and it shouldn't become one.
